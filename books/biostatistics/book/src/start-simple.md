# Choose Your Path

Statistics can be learned at two useful depths. You do not need to choose the
"hard" route to prove anything. Choose the route that answers your present
question.

## Route A: Start Simple

Choose **Start Simple** when you want intuition first, are returning after a
long break, or need to read a health study without becoming a statistician.

```text
10 short lessons
       |
       +-- centre and spread
       +-- uncertainty and p-values
       +-- disease rates and 2x2 tables
       +-- study designs, bias, and screening
       +-- reading evidence
```

Each lesson takes about 15-25 minutes and contains:

- one practical question;
- one small visual;
- one calculation explained in words;
- one independent BioLang example;
- one sentence you could use to report the result;
- one warning about what the result cannot establish.

Start with [Lesson 1: Centre](simple-01-centre.md).

## Route B: The Full 30-Day Course

Choose the full course when you need to design or defend an analysis, work with
omics data, fit models, check assumptions, or produce research results.

It includes t-tests, non-parametric tests, ANOVA, multiple testing, regression,
survival analysis, power, PCA, clustering, resampling, Bayesian reasoning,
meta-analysis, reproducibility, and three capstones.

Start with [Day 1: Why Statistics?](day-01.md).

## A Sensible Combined Route

If you are new, use this order:

```text
Start Simple 1-10
        |
        v
Full Days 1-7
        |
        +-- stop here for statistical literacy
        |
        v
Choose later days for your actual analysis
```

You do not need to read all 30 full chapters in sequence. The end of each short
lesson points to the most relevant full chapters.

## What This Book Will Not Do

It will not choose a test solely from the shape of a histogram, declare an
unusual observation to be an error, turn association into causation, or treat
`p < 0.05` as automatic proof. Statistical calculations are only trustworthy
when the question, measurements, sampling, and study design make sense.

> **The central habit:** first ask what was measured and how the observations
> were obtained. Only then ask which calculation to use.
